Deciphering Traffic Dynamics: Analysing the Induced Traffic of the Padma Multipurpose Bridge

A mixed-methods transport study that estimates a present-day no-bridge counterfactual, compares it with observed bridge traffic, interprets behavioural change and tests long-term traffic scenarios under uncertainty.

Field: Transportation planning & geographyMethods: Counterfactual analysis · GIS · qualitative interviews · Monte Carlo simulationSupervisor: Professor Dr. Md Ahsanul Kabir

Research problem

The Padma Bridge replaced a ferry- and launch-dependent crossing system with a fixed road connection. Post-opening traffic rose sharply, but traffic observed on the bridge cannot be interpreted as induced travel by itself: some movement reflects ordinary growth, route diversion, mode transfer and previously suppressed demand. The dissertation therefore asks what cross-Padma traffic would likely have looked like without the bridge before interpreting the difference.

Mixed-methods counterfactual design

The quantitative component combines temporal and spatial counterfactual reasoning. Pre-bridge movement trends provide the principal temporal no-bridge estimate, while district-level built-up area and census information support spatial interpretation and sensitivity checks. Monte Carlo simulation represents uncertainty rather than presenting the counterfactual as a single perfectly known value. A qualitative interview component is used to interpret whether reported behavioural changes are consistent with additional travel, destination change, mode transfer or route diversion.

The numerical difference is described as bridge-attributable excess traffic, not as pure induced traffic. That distinction is deliberate: the counterfactual allows ordinary pre-bridge movement to grow, but the remaining excess can still include both newly generated travel and redistributed movement.

Behavioural interpretation

Interview evidence indicates several mechanisms operating at the same time. Respondents reported increased trip frequency, trips that became newly feasible, destination changes, mode transfer and route diversion. Frequency-induced travel and newly feasible trips provide the clearest behavioural evidence of additional travel, while route and mode shifts show why the entire excess should not be labelled induced demand.

Thirty-year scenario forecast

The dissertation uses scenario-based forecasting rather than a single deterministic prediction. Under alternative assumptions about the persistence of induced demand, the 2054 traffic range is approximately 33,169 to 44,537 vehicles per day. The range is intended for planning interpretation and sensitivity testing, not as a claim that one exact future traffic value is known today.

Interpretation and limitations

The present no-bridge condition is unobservable, so the result depends on counterfactual assumptions and the quality of historical movement, spatial and traffic data. Built-up area is a supporting proxy rather than direct proof of travel generation, and the qualitative component supports mechanism interpretation rather than population-level decomposition of every vehicle. The study therefore treats uncertainty explicitly and keeps bridge-attributable excess traffic conceptually separate from pure induced demand.
